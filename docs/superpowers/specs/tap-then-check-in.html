Tap, Then Check In

The button does nothing until a sticker has been touched. That single rule is the whole mechanism — everything below is what happens either side of it.

The one rule

Three states, in order

The same three states on both apps. A student marking a register and a member of staff clocking in see the identical sequence — only the words on the button differ.

Before Check in

Grey and unpressable. Nothing happens if you tap it, and nothing is sent anywhere.

Sticker touched Check in

Sticker read — 54s left

The button comes alive and a countdown starts. Sixty seconds by default — long enough to press, too short to walk to another room.

Pressed ✓  Marked present · 09:07

Saved immediately, with the minute. Nobody has to approve it and no lecturer has to press anything first.

Why the countdown exists

Without it, a sticker touched in the corridor at nine o’clock would still open the button at eleven. The window is what keeps a tap meaning “I am here now” rather than “I walked past this once today”. When it lapses the button greys out and the reading is discarded — not kept for later.

Staff

Clocking in

Same three states, one difference: the choice of where you are working comes first, and it decides whether a sticker is needed at all.

Onsite Sticker required

Onsite→ Clock in→ touch sticker→ Clock in

The ordinary button is replaced, not sat beside. There is no second route in — the server refuses an onsite clock-in that did not come from a sticker, so knowing the address of the old one does not help.

Online No sticker

Online→ Clock in

Never gated, deliberately. A sticker proves somebody stood in the building, which is exactly what a person working from home cannot produce — requiring one would make the honest answer the only unrecordable one.

A tap records Onsite, and is not asked

Someone who touched a sticker in the building cannot be working remotely. The tap route does not offer the choice and the server ignores it if sent, because recording anything else would store a claim the tap itself disproves.

The one thing that differs by phone

Android is silent. iPhone needs one press.

Apple always shows its own scan sheet and it cannot be suppressed — a platform rule, not something in this code. Worth seeing before a demo.

Android As pictured

Check in→ hold to sticker→ Check in

The app listens quietly the whole time the screen is open. The student does one thing.

iPhone One extra press

Check in Scan→ Apple’s sheet→ sticker→ Check in

Still quick, but not silent. An iPhone also cannot read a blank sticker at all, so stickers registered by tapping a blank one must be registered on Android.

When it says no

Every refusal, and what it means

Each one is recorded against the sticker, so “I tapped and nothing happened” is a question with an answer.

What they seeWhyWhat to do
No class of yours is running right now They tapped before the class started or after it ended. The lecture’s own hours are the window. Nothing — the card names the next class and its start time.
Two of your classes are running Their timetable genuinely overlaps, so only they can say which room they are in. They pick one, then press.
That class is outside its teaching term The term has ended or has not started. Registers close with the term. An administrator reopens the term to correct a mark.
You were already marked A mark exists — theirs from an earlier tap, or one the lecturer set. Nothing. A tap never overwrites a lecturer’s mark.
That tag isn’t recognised The sticker was never registered, or has been retired. Register it, or restore it, on the NFC Tags tab.
Nothing appears at all The college has not switched tap check-in on. Students are not shown a feature their college has not adopted. Switch it on — step 1 below.

What a tap does not prove

That somebody was at that wall once, with their account signed in — not that they are there now. A sticker’s code is fixed and any phone can read it, so a student who notes one down could mark themselves present from home. The lecturer’s live screen and the tap log are what make that visible afterwards; nothing prevents it. It is a real improvement on a button anyone could press, and it is not proof of presence.

Meanwhile, on the dashboard

Two new tabs

Live Attendance

The register filling in as it happens: present, late, and who has not appeared, with the minute each of them arrived. A My classes / All classes switch, and one button at the end that marks everyone still unmarked as absent — naming the number before it does it. That button is the only thing in the whole feature that records an absence, and a person has to press it.

NFC Tags

Register a sticker, retire one that has peeled off, bring it back later, and read every tap it has taken — refusals included. Also where the feature is switched on, and where the lateness threshold and the sixty-second window are set.

To make it work

Five things, in this order

  1. Switch it on

    NFC Tags tab → Tap check-in is ON. It ships off, so until this is done students see nothing and staff keep the ordinary buttons.

  2. Register a sticker

    On an Android phone: name the room, press Tap a blank sticker, hold it against one. At a desk: press Generate a code, and write it onto the sticker with an NFC writing app. The code is shown once and never again.

  3. Put it on the wall

    One per room. Plain, pennies-each stickers are fine.

  4. Make sure students can sign in

    A student cannot check in without a password. They claim an account and an administrator approves it, or an administrator sets one at the desk on the Passwords page.

  5. Get the app onto a phone

    This is the one nothing can substitute for. Tapping is a native-app feature — a browser cannot read a sticker — so it needs the installed Android or iOS app. No physical tap has been performed yet.

Written 29 August 2026 for London Brookes. Everything above is built and tested through the API; the tap itself has never been tried against a real sticker on a real handset.
